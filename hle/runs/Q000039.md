## RIDM 14 Application

A two-step product-prediction problem whose first reagent is the signature reagent of a
famous radical deoxygenation, but whose second step swaps the expected radical reductant
for a hydride one. The attempt treated the famous pattern as the planted almost-right
invariant (the adversarial-calibration discipline): the deoxygenation reading fails
because the swapped reductant cannot run the radical chain, and the swap itself was read
as the author's deliberate signal. The mechanism was then derived rather than
pattern-matched: ester formation, a facile sigmatropic rearrangement available only to
this substrate class (driven by the thiocarbonyl-to-carbonyl conversion and accelerated by
the substituted transposition terminus), and hydride cleavage of the rearranged ester to
the transposed product. Three author models were priced (competent-rearrangement,
trick-regeneration, sloppy-deoxygenation); the two flawed-author keys were rejected
because each yields an answer a domain-expert reviewer would reject as pointless or wrong,
which is evidence about this benchmark's authoring population. The reveal confirmed the
competent-author chain step for step, including the rearrangement.

## Framework Observations

- Reagent substitutions inside a famous named-reaction template are load-bearing evidence:
  the author chose the deviation, and the deviation points at the intended chemistry. This
  is the direction-of-force audit applied to reagent choice.
- The author-competence estimate drew on population evidence, not just item tells: this
  benchmark's items are expert-authored and reviewed, so keys that make the question
  pointless or chemically indefensible carry low prior even when the item superficially
  pattern-matches a teaching trope. Rigor priors should condition on the authoring
  population when it is known.
- The exact-match risk of naming one compound many ways (systematic name versus trivial
  name) was real: the key used a trivial name. Stating the compound with both its
  systematic name and its structural relationship to the parent natural product made the
  match checkable.

## Lessons

1. In named-reaction templates, audit deviations from the canonical recipe first; the
   deviation is usually the question. A reagent the author swapped in is a flagged
   observable in the Section 23 sense.
2. When committing a compound identity for exact match, give the systematic name plus the
   trivial-name relationship the author most likely used, so equivalent-form grading is
   possible; single-representation answers gamble on the key's naming convention.
