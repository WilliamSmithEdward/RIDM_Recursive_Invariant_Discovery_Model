## RIDM 30 Application

A one-line diagram of an industrial network with transformer and generator ratings
but no load values, a stem demanding a calculation of the grid's real-power supply
under vaguely stated loss conditions, and a five-option menu whose options pair a
power value with a sentence about harmonic-resonance impact. The item was typed
generated: the calculation is not derivable from the figure, two loss conditions are
stated without parameters, and the options carry delimiter artifacts from an editing
pass. A bounded search over simple expressions in the figure's ratings found no
natural expression landing on any option value at better than the search's own
chance-match rate, confirming that the values were not anchored to the figure.

The licensed instrument was seed centrality. The first slot's value shared by three
of five options was read as the seed, correctly. The second slot was then filtered by
a clause-consistency rule: the stem presupposed that both losses and voltage
stability degrade despite compensation, and the three options containing a clause
contradicting that presupposition were killed. The survivors were the central
percentage and its upward perturbation; the central one was committed at the low
band with the pool-filter alarm bound.

The key was one of the killed options: the seed value paired with a slightly lower
percentage and a clause the stem's own framing contradicts. The key's rationale
reaches the seed value by summing generator capacities as demand, dividing by the
nominal power factor, and adding three invented loss terms; its harmonic percentage
is described as an empirical figure, the rationale names a different percentage in
the next paragraph, and it states that voltage instability worsens while the keyed
option says stability is unaffected. Those are internal inconsistencies in the key,
noted as key quality only; grading is strict.

## Framework Observations

- Candidate coverage held: the keyed option was listed as a priced variant before
  commitment and killed by a rule.
- The killing rule was unlicensed for the class. Clause-level falsity was evaluated
  against the stem's own framing, which is a coherence instrument; the generator that
  wrote the key never checked the key against the stem, so stem-option consistency
  has no predictive force on a generated menu. The framework's rule that the rigor
  class licenses the instrument was applied for seed centrality and then bypassed for
  the clause filter.
- The pool filter was applied at narrative tier on the argument that an incoherent
  stem scatters the reference population. The stem's calculation was incoherent, but
  the menu's structure was a funnel: a value repeated across three options plus one
  option matching the stem's framing sends every solver, model or human, to the same
  option. My own first pass landed there in a few lines, which was the observation
  that the population converges. Convergence is predicted from the menu's funnel, not
  from the stem's computability, and the item's survival then keyed off the funnel's
  target, as the rule already says.
- Two fired signals pointed at the keyed option and were outweighed by one unlicensed
  rule: the filter's prediction that the key sits off the modal answer, and the stem's
  request for a percentage, which, among the seed-value options the funnel did not
  select, leaves exactly one.

## Lessons

- On a generated menu, stem-option consistency demotes nothing: the generator's key
  was never checked against its own stem, so a clause that contradicts the stem's
  framing is not false in the author's referent. Only seed centrality and the filter
  are licensed there.
- The reference population's convergence is read from the menu's funnel structure, a
  repeated value plus a stem-echoing clause, not from whether the stem's calculation
  is coherent; the option my own quick elimination selects is the modal answer, and
  the filter keys off it.
