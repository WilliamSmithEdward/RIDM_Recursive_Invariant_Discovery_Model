## RIDM 29 Application

A middlegame position given in standard notation, with the question
of how many moves White needs to win under optimal play. The item
was typed well-posed with an engine as the author's likely referent.
Hand analysis found forcing lines but no short mate, so the rigorous
route became instrumentation: a move-generation library was
installed into the session scratchpad and a mate-distance solver
written on top of it, then rewritten in C when the Python version
proved too slow past seven moves. The solver was anchored before
being trusted, twice: its move generator reproduced the library's
perft counts exactly on three positions covering en passant and
promotion, and its search reproduced known mate distances on four
anchor positions. The faster rewrite failed one anchor, which
exposed a transposition-table bug (a checkmate proven with zero
moves remaining was stored as no information); the bug was fixed and
the anchors re-run before any live result was used. Exhaustive
search then proved that no mate exists in eight or fewer moves. A
chess engine, installed as a WebAssembly package and driven over its
standard protocol, reported mate in eleven, then ten, then nine as
its depth grew; the nine-move line was checked as a legal
checkmating sequence, and the exhaustive solver confirmed a forced
mate in nine. The committed value was nine, with ten and eleven
computed pre-reveal as the values a standard engine displays at
moderate depth, the one mechanism by which the key could differ.

## Framework Observations

- Anchoring the self-built instrument was decisive. The faster
  solver's anchor failure would otherwise have been invisible, and
  it was a soundness bug that could have produced a wrong "no mate"
  verdict at the live depth.
- The convergent-route rule was satisfied by two instruments sharing
  nothing: an exhaustive AND/OR search for the lower bound and a
  heuristic engine for the witness, each checked against the other.
- Referent pricing was the right frame: the key's own rationale
  says the engine must run for a while to reach the optimal value,
  which is exactly the depth-convergence variant family priced
  before the reveal. The band stayed medium because that family,
  not the derivation, was the weakest decisive link.

## Lessons

No new failure lesson. Win pattern: on a game-tree item, prove the
lower bound exhaustively and exhibit the witness with a second
instrument, anchor every self-built tool on known cases before the
live case, and price the referent's convergence variants rather
than assuming the author's tool ran to optimality.
